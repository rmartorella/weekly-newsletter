One

What we are seeing

Consumer stopped buying demand and started buying supply.

We read sixty consumer briefs end to end this month and the striking thing was not what was in them. It was what was missing. No campaign development. No innovation pipeline. No direct-to-consumer growth builds. Essentially no brand work of any kind. What was there instead was somebody sitting inside a forecasting, allocation or order-to-cash process making it work, usually in the wake of a half-finished planning or ERP go-live. One brief asked, by name, for low ego. Another said flatly that the client does not seek recommendations or theories.

Absence is the hardest thing to notice and usually the most useful. A trade publication can tell you what is being written about. Only we can tell you what has quietly stopped being bought, because we are the ones getting the briefs that no longer mention it. We are going to make this a regular feature, because in every read we have done so far the missing category has been more informative than the growing one.

Pat Petitti
Two

The data

Demand trends

Zoom out far enough and one force explains most of the rest. The deal cycle is now the clock the entire market runs on. Around six in ten briefs come from a private equity firm or a PE-backed company, integration and carve-out language have both roughly doubled in two years, and the buying unit has shifted from a scoped deliverable toward a person in a seat, because a deal needs somebody in place rather than a recommendation. AI is riding on top of that rather than beside it. The second exhibit below is the one we watch hardest, because it is the only thing we have that leads rather than lags.

Read it as: the AI line and the interim line converging in the last two quarters is the whole story of how this market buys now. Clients are buying AI capability and they are buying it as a person, not as a programme. The private equity line sitting above both is the reason: a sponsor with a hold period does not have time to run a programme.
So whatPosition yourself as a person, not a programme. The briefs increasingly describe a seat someone will sit in rather than a deliverable someone will hand over, so lead with what you have run rather than what you have produced.

Being visible before the scoping happens is worth far more than being available after it.

Concentration: where the market actually isMinimum quarterly

Type and industry combined

Our briefs landed in 53 distinct project types across 8 industries this year, which is 234 live pockets of demand. Only about a fifth of those pockets saw five or more projects all year, and only ten saw one a month. The concentration is extreme: due diligence in sponsor-backed businesses, process improvement in industrials, and post-merger integration across both account for a wildly disproportionate share of everything transacted. If you sit outside those pockets, volume is not your friend and specificity is.

Why this matters to you: it explains why a generalist profile performs badly here. In a market of 234 small pockets the winning move is to be the obvious answer in two of them, not a plausible answer in forty.
So whatPick two pockets and be the obvious answer in both. A profile that is plausible across forty categories performs worse here than one that is unarguable in two, because the matching step rewards specificity over range.

Buyer decision trends

When we ask clients what made an engagement good, they almost never mention the analysis. They talk about responsiveness, clear direction, iteration speed, and fitting into the team. And the failure mode runs the same way: engagements that go wrong go wrong on format and availability, not on thinking. That is a cheap correction with a large payoff, and it is the reason extensions get decided in a room you were never invited to.

So whatSend your sponsor a one page executive summary in week one, before anyone asks for it. And edit everything that leaves your desk so it does not read as machine output, because that is now a stated reason for ending engagements.

Supply trends

We compared the 144 consultants who won three or more projects this year against the 3,878 who were matched to five or more briefs and won nothing. We expected experience and price to separate them. Neither does. What separates them is whether the profile is finished and current, which is both the least glamorous finding we have published and the most actionable.

Outcomes

A note on budgets, because this is where we see the most value left on the table. Clients frequently post a band they are not confident in, and the final contracted value often lands somewhere quite different once scope is properly defined. The consultants who do well here treat the stated budget as an opening position rather than a constraint, and scope a small first phase that makes starting cheap.

Overall project value: the stated budget is an opening positionMinimum monthly

Posted band versus contracted value

A large share of briefs arrive with the budget marked uncertain. Where clients do state a band up front, the contracted value regularly lands outside it once scope firms up, and it moves in both directions. What separates the engagements that land above the posted band is almost always the same thing: the consultant reframed the scope before the number was discussed, rather than negotiating the number afterwards.

How to use it: if a brief says the budget is uncertain, that is not a warning sign. It is an invitation to define the work, and whoever defines the work usually sets the price.
So whatWhen a brief says the budget is uncertain, lead with scope rather than price. Propose the shape of the work first and let the number follow it. Whoever defines the work usually ends up setting the price.

The Bet

Every quarter we put three things on the record about where we think the market goes next, and the last issue of the quarter grades the previous set in public. A forecast is only worth reading if somebody keeps score.

How last quarter’s bets did

—
HeldIntegration would stop being an org chart problem and become an application portfolio problem.
Called in Q2. The named workstream in these briefs is now application migration and rationalisation, run as a delivery portfolio, across three sectors.
—
PartlyInterim demand would keep climbing through the summer.
It climbed harder than we forecast. We attributed it to cost pressure; the driver turned out to be delayed and failed permanent searches, which points to a different set of opportunities. Direction right, mechanism wrong.
—
MissedSustainability and ESG work would rebound as reporting deadlines landed.
It has not. Across consumer and energy briefs this year the category is close to absent, and where it appears it is a change programme by another name. Marked missed.

Our three bets for this quarter

1
TSA exit becomes a specialism clients search for by name.
Carve-out and TSA language has doubled as a share of briefs in two years and is now rising faster than integration itself. It is showing up independently in four sectors. We will know by the end of the quarter whether it survives the next deal cycle.
2
The data centre supply chain gets recognised as its own vertical.
Cooling, thermal management, logistics, fuel sourcing, power. It is arriving filed under industrials, technology and energy at the same time, which is usually what a new vertical looks like right before somebody names it.
3
Fixed fee passes half of all engagements.
Hourly fell five and a half points this year and fixed fee overtook it for the first time. If the trend holds its slope, deliverable and retainer structures together cross fifty per cent inside two quarters, and the pricing conversation changes with it.
